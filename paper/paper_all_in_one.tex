\begin{abstract}
Delegated purchasing must respect the user's authorization even when a request admits several interpretations or contractual outcomes remain uncertain. We formulate risk-aware purchase or refusal by separating authorization across retained states from worst-case utility. Set implements this formulation, selecting a jointly admissible purchase with the highest positive worst-case value, or refusing when none qualifies. This permits useful decisions without further user interaction. We analyze why a safe selected action can coexist with an incorrect authorization domain: omitted states can weaken permissions and inflate guaranteed value, while ranking can conceal the resulting errors. On synthetic contractual-boundary tasks across multiple model families, Set improves exact decisions and useful completion while reducing unsafe acceptances and false refusals relative to direct decisions under the same stated policy. Controlled intent tests examine unresolved reference and scope. Frozen-rule replays and selected-output interventions show how changes in candidate availability or utility expose permissions that safe current purchases leave undetected.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Language agents use external tools to carry out multi-step tasks \citep{react,schick2023toolformer}. Benchmarks cover shopping, travel, and service workflows \citep{webshop,travelplanner,taubench}, as well as broader web tasks \citep{zhou2024webarena,deng2023mind2web}. ST-WebAgentBench counts completion as successful only when applicable policies are satisfied \citep{stwebagentbench}. In delegated purchasing, the commitment and its possible consequences must stay within the user's authorization. Cancellation, return, or replacement costs can exceed that authorization even when the initial price is within budget.

Uncertainty complicates this judgment in two ways. Different readings of a request can impose different requirements, and future events determine which financial consequences occur. We study purchase or refusal without another user turn, using only the authorization already given. Choosing a convenient interpretation can permit an unauthorized purchase; refusing every uncertain request can discard useful options. Yet a unique interpretation is not always needed: one purchase may satisfy every retained possibility. The task is to find such a common authorized purchase with positive worst-case value.

Clarification and uncertainty-aware planning study how additional information can improve decisions \citep{knowno,voi,sage}. Risk-Averse Calibration (RAC) connects uncertainty sets to consequence-sensitive selection \citep{rac}. We use its fixed-set max--min principle, with purchasing permissions that cannot be traded against utility. Applying this principle to language-based delegation requires preserving which conditions apply under each reading or event. A loss limit, for example, restricts what the user permits; it is not compensation that reduces the loss. Such distinctions determine both whether the agent may act and which authorized action it should take.

The resulting decisions raise a second question: what does a safe purchase tell us about the agent's understanding of authorization? A rule may admit an unauthorized offer but rank a safe one above it. The current action then conceals the permission error. If the safe offer becomes unavailable, or the ranking changes, the same rule may choose the unauthorized alternative. Conversely, an overly restrictive rule can prevent useful purchases. Studying these cases requires examining permitted alternatives and their values alongside the selected action.

We introduce \ours\ to apply these authorization and value criteria consistently. Its fixed procedure evaluates all retained states before ranking admissible purchases by worst-case utility. Holding the conditions fixed lets us examine how candidate availability and value affect selection. Our experiments evaluate the complete workflow across model families, then use controlled ambiguity tests, catalogue replays, and component interventions to investigate when errors become visible. The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Risk-aware decisions under unresolved uncertainty:} a formulation of purchase or refusal that requires a common authorized action and separates its permission from its worst-case value.
\item \textbf{Analysis of hidden authorization errors:} we distinguish action safety from authorization-domain soundness, derive the effects of omitted states, and explain how availability and ranking can conceal or expose permission errors.
\item \textbf{Controlled empirical investigation:} multi-model boundary and intent tests measure unsafe acceptance and missed purchases; frozen-rule replays and component interventions examine the mechanisms behind apparently safe outcomes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Decisions under uncertainty.}
KnowNo and AmbiK address ambiguous planning instructions \citep{knowno,ambik}; IntentSim evaluates clarification across language tasks \citep{intentsim}. AmbigQA and AmbiEnt examine multiple readings in question answering and natural language inference \citep{min2020ambigqa,liu2023ambiguity}. VoI weighs information against user effort, and SAGE represents uncertainty over tool parameters \citep{voi,sage}. We study purchase or refusal without another user turn. RAC supplies a max--min rule over prediction sets; related methods study calibrated risk guarantees \citep{rac,crc,actionrac}. We apply the fixed-set principle to purchasing permissions and a common action across states, with refusal as the outside option. Our analysis does not add a calibration guarantee.

\paragraph{Policy reasoning and decision conditions.}
Complex-policy studies identify workflow-governing rules as a major reasoning difficulty \citep{complexpolicy}. AgentIF examines conditional constraints and rules governing which constraints apply and take priority \citep{agentif}. These findings motivate attention to relationships among conditions. Executable reasoning separates interpretation from computation \citep{pal,logiclm,linc}; semantic parsing captures compositional requests and constrains their representation \citep{gupta2018hierarchical,shin2021constrained}. Genie maps assistant commands to ThingTalk programs \citep{campagna2019genie}, while financial contract languages define interpretation and valuation rules \citep{composingcontracts}. Formalization studies examine translation errors \citep{folcode,fover,verify}. We study their consequences for authorization and worst-case value under retained readings and events.

\paragraph{Agent safeguards and policy compliance.}
ToolEmu and ToolSword evaluate risks in tool use \citep{toolemu,ye2024toolsword}; AgentDojo and AgentHarm study prompt injection and harmful tasks \citep{agentdojo,agentharm}. GuardAgent, DeAction, AgentSpec, and VeriGuard generate or enforce behavioral constraints \citep{guardagent,deaction,agentspec,veriguard}. AgentAuditor assesses evaluator reliability, using strict and lenient standards for ambiguous risk judgments \citep{agentauditor}. Our experiments use explicit purchasing permissions and assess both unauthorized acceptance and missed authorized purchases.

\paragraph{Errors hidden by outcomes.}
OpenAgentSafety distinguishes safe behavior from unsuccessful unsafe attempts and failures before exposure to risk \citep{openagentsafety}. Near-Miss, published at the GEM workshop, detects missing information checks before state-changing actions even when the final outcome is correct \citep{nearmiss}. These studies complement challenge sets, behavioral tests, and contrast sets \citep{mccoy2019right,checklist,contrastsets}. We examine a different mechanism: a rule already admits an unauthorized purchase, but ranking or candidate availability prevents its selection. Frozen-rule replays and component interventions expose this error; our state-omission analysis relates its permission and value effects.
\section{Risk-Aware Delegation under Uncertainty}
\label{sec:method}
\label{sec:contract}
We define which purchases a delegation permits before deciding which is most valuable. The same commitment must remain acceptable across the possibilities retained from the request and its contractual context.

\subsection{Authorization and Value across States}
\label{sec:formulation}
Let $x=(P,c)$ contain a natural-language request and context, and let $z\in C\subseteq\Z$ be one complete purchase in a finite catalogue. A nonempty $\Theta(x)$ contains retained interpretations, relevant future events, or their combinations. Interpretations determine what the user requires; events determine the consequences of committing to a purchase. We evaluate the same purchase under each state without further user interaction or a probability distribution over states.

For state $\theta$, let $H_\theta(x,z)$ encode hard requirements, $\ell_\theta(x,z)$ financial consequences, $\A_\theta(x)$ the authorized risk region, and $u_\theta(x,z)$ utility on a common cardinal scale. Define
\begin{align}
F_\theta(x)&=\{z:H_\theta(x,z)=1,\ \ell_\theta(x,z)\in\A_\theta(x)\},\label{eq:state}\\
F(x)&=\bigcap_{\theta\in\Theta(x)}F_\theta(x),\label{eq:common}\\
\psi_x(z)&=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta(x)}u_\theta(x,z).\label{eq:robust}
\end{align}
Authorization requires one purchase to satisfy every retained state: $\exists z\,\forall\theta$. Separate purchases that work under separate readings do not meet this requirement. With refusal worth zero, the decision solves
\begin{equation}
\max_{z\in C\cap F(x)}\psi_x(z).
\label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
The agent selects a maximizer only if this maximum is positive. It refuses when the domain is empty or the maximum is nonpositive. Utility ranks authorized purchases; it cannot justify violating a hard requirement or increasing a financial permission. This instantiates RAC's fixed-set max--min principle \citep{rac} with explicit authorization and an outside option.

\paragraph{A common purchase need not resolve meaning.}
In our ticket tasks, Zhang and Li need tickets within 3,000 monetary units (MU), and ``his ticket'' must allow a fee-free refund. Two 2,400-MU orders refunding only one traveller each are separately feasible but provide no common purchase. Adding a 2,800-MU order refunding both permits action, with stipulated utility $(3000-2800)/1000=1/5$. The ambiguity remains. Buying the common order relies on the existing budget and value conditions, not additional authorization (Appendix~\ref{app:observed-trace}).

These equations define the target semantics used for offline references. Experimental inputs state events, permissions, and utility conventions, but do not supply their executable reference expressions. Errors in deriving the conditions, including omitted readings and incorrect clause binding, remain part of the end-to-end evaluation.

\subsection{Purchase, Refusal, and Incomplete Evaluation}
\label{sec:realization}
\label{sec:contracts}
\label{sec:intent-dsl}
Let $Q$ denote the executable decision conditions used by \ours, with hats marking quantities evaluated from them. A clause supplies a hard predicate $\widehat H_j$, financial permission $\widehat R_j$, and utility $\widehat u_j$ for one retained state. Their conjunction defines the admitted catalogue domain $\widehat F_C$; their minimum gives $\widehat\psi$. Section~\ref{sec:setup} specifies the experimental inputs and execution protocol. Algorithm~\ref{alg:decision} applies a fixed selection rule to them.

The procedure exactly solves the compiled version of Equation~\eqref{eq:decision} on a complete, resolved finite catalogue. Every candidate's admissibility, and every admitted candidate's state utilities, must be known. A feasible offer cannot be certified optimal while a competitor has unknown value. Correctness relative to the request additionally requires the decision conditions to preserve its target semantics.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Risk-aware purchase selection}
\label{alg:decision}
\small
\textbf{Input:} request/context $x$, schema $\Sigma$, catalogue $C$\par
\textbf{Output:} \textsc{Accept}, \textsc{Refuse}, or \textsc{Fail}, with diagnostics\par\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}r@{\quad}p{.88\columnwidth}@{}}
1 & $Q\leftarrow f(x,\Sigma)$ \quad\emph{derive conditions; no $C$}\\
2 & Validate the generated conditions and their types.\\
3 & If invalid, return \textsc{Fail}(compilation).\\
4 & Evaluate every generated state on every $z\in C$; retain the condition/value matrix.\\
5 & If execution is invalid, return \textsc{Fail}(execution).\\
6 & For each $z\in C$, set $\widehat f(z)=\bigwedge_j(\widehat H_j(z)\wedge\widehat R_j(z))$.\\
7 & If catalogue coverage or any $\widehat f(z)$ is unresolved, or a candidate with $\widehat f(z)=1$ has an unknown state utility, return \textsc{Fail}(uncertified).\\
8 & $\widehat F_C\leftarrow\{z\in C:\widehat f(z)=1\}$.\\
9 & If $\widehat F_C=\varnothing$, return \textsc{Refuse}(infeasible).\\
10 & For $z\in\widehat F_C$, set $\widehat\psi(z)=\min_j\widehat u_j(z)$.\\
11 & $v\leftarrow\max_{z\in\widehat F_C}\widehat\psi(z)$.\\
12 & If $v\le0$, return \textsc{Refuse}(nonpositive value).\\
13 & Return \textsc{Accept}($\{z\in\widehat F_C:\widehat\psi(z)=v\},v$).\\
\end{tabular}\smallskip\par
\emph{Fixed evaluator: lines 2--13. Reference answers are used only in offline evaluation.}
\end{algorithm}

\textsc{Refuse} establishes that the evaluated conditions admit no positive-value solution. \textsc{Fail} means the required compilation or evaluation did not complete. Both prevent purchase, but only the former is a decision about the evaluated domain. The procedure computes all maximizers; the response may name a nonempty subset of tied optima with their correct value, from which one complete purchase is chosen. Exhaustive evaluation costs $O(MKT)$ for $M$ candidates, $K$ generated states, and expression cost $T$. Appendix~\ref{app:interface} gives the execution rules and guarantee conditions.
\section{Why Safe Choices Can Hide Authorization Errors}
\label{sec:analysis}
\label{sec:verification}
Authorization determines which purchases enter the choice set; utility determines which of them is selected. An error in either quantity can change the decision, but errors can also conceal one another.

\subsection{Selected Actions and Admitted Domains}
Write $F_C=C\cap F(x)$ for the reference-authorized domain. A selected purchase $z^\star$ is safe when $z^\star\in F_C$. Soundness of the generated authorization domain is stronger:
\begin{equation}
\widehat F_C\subseteq F_C.
\label{eq:domain-soundness}
\end{equation}
The difference $\widehat F_C\setminus F_C$ contains unauthorized offers the rule admits, including those never selected. Checking one safe action cannot establish that this difference is empty. Moreover, safety alone does not establish an exact decision: the purchase must maximize reference worst-case utility and report its value correctly.

The reverse difference, $F_C\setminus\widehat F_C$, contains authorized offers the rule excludes. Such exclusions can cause false refusal or remove the best purchase. A rule admitting nothing is sound by Equation~\eqref{eq:domain-soundness}, yet may fail to complete an authorized task. Refusal is correct only when no reference-authorized purchase has positive guaranteed value. Our evaluation therefore checks useful completion and refusal alongside unsafe acceptance and admitted domains.

\subsection{Omitted States Affect Permission and Value}
For fixed $x$, consider a nonempty retained subset $\Theta'\subseteq\Theta$, with all remaining state functions unchanged. Equations~\eqref{eq:common} and~\eqref{eq:robust} imply
\begin{equation}
F_\Theta\subseteq F_{\Theta'},\qquad
\psi_{\Theta'}(z)\ge\psi_\Theta(z).
\label{eq:omission}
\end{equation}
Removing conjuncts cannot shrink the admissible set, and removing terms cannot lower the worst-case value. A single omission can thus weaken authorization and inflate the value used for selection. Both inequalities can be equalities: a redundant state changes neither quantity. The relevant issue is whether its conditions affect the aggregate, not the number of labels emitted. Equivalent merged conditions preserve the decision semantics.

An interpretation can bind several quantities at once. In the hotel tasks, ``his room'' links the same guest to an arrival-dependent cancellation deadline, the fee checked against the authorized cap, and the utility penalty. Omitting that reading can remove both a permission check and a source of low value. Binding those quantities to different guests is another error; Equation~\eqref{eq:omission} applies specifically to deletion with the other state functions held fixed. Appendix~\ref{app:intent} gives the task semantics.

\subsection{Availability and Ranking Can Mask Permission Errors}
Suppose a fixed rule admits two offers, a reference-safe $a$ and an unauthorized $b$, with
\begin{equation}
\widehat\psi(a)>\widehat\psi(b)>0.
\label{eq:masking}
\end{equation}
If these are the only admitted offers, the rule selects $a$. Its safe choice leaves the permission for $b$ untested. Removing $a$ makes $b$ the positive-value maximizer without changing either offer's conditions. More generally, a singleton catalogue exposes any unauthorized offer that the frozen rule admits with positive estimated value. An admitted offer with nonpositive value need not be selected, even when it is the only option. Domain errors and unsafe purchases therefore need separate measurements.

The same separation explains component interventions. Replacing utility by a function $v$ for which $v(b)>\max\{0,v(a)\}$ changes the choice to $b$ while leaving permission unchanged. Even a correct utility function cannot repair an overbroad authorization domain: an unauthorized purchase can have high task value. Conversely, correcting permission can prevent an unsafe choice while leaving incorrect values or rankings among the remaining offers.

These observations motivate two checks in Section~\ref{sec:permissions}. Catalogue replays hold generated rules and the selector fixed while changing available offers. Component interventions substitute reference permission or utility separately. They test whether availability and ranking conceal an existing permission error; their selected cases establish a mechanism rather than its prevalence in deployment.

\subsection{Tasks, Comparisons, and Scoring}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Contractual boundaries.}
We construct 200 tasks from 20 templates in four families: coupon cancellation, insured cancellation, threshold-dependent returns, and service replacement. Contracts combine overlapping groups, compensation layers, shipping charges, and item/order caps. Each purchase has six components; all subsets of up to four affected components give 57 events, including the empty event. Catalogues contain 128--1,024 candidates. One MU equals 100 integer subunits.

Each template contributes five feasible/infeasible pairs. A feasible task has exactly one authorized positive-value candidate. Increasing its fee by the feasible margin plus one subunit puts its worst loss exactly one subunit above the limit; other attributes and offer order remain fixed. The pairs isolate responsiveness to authorization boundaries \citep{checklist,contrastsets}. Separate cash-ledger and expression implementations agree on 6,712,320 candidate--event checks, supplemented by 25,600 random-attribute checks and three hand-calculated witnesses. Inputs and references were frozen before primary calls (Appendix~\ref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Decision workflows.}
\direct\ receives the request, schema, catalogue, and the joint-feasibility, worst-value, and refusal rules of Section~\ref{sec:method}. It returns a decision and utility without external execution. \ours\ separates preparation of the state-dependent conditions from fixed catalogue evaluation. The preparation stage receives the request and schema but excludes the catalogue. Conditions use exact arithmetic and retain clause scope, compensation order, and value binding. Evaluation semantics and observed compilations are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The shared decision policy fixes the target behavior for both methods; the comparison measures how reliably each complete workflow realizes that behavior. Appendix~\ref{app:execution} reports the textual-checklist baseline and sensitivity analyses.

\paragraph{Outcomes.}
An \emph{exact decision} agrees with the reference and, for acceptance, returns authorized maximizers with correct utility. \emph{Safe completion} is exact acceptance on a feasible task. \emph{Unsafe acceptance} selects a reference-infeasible offer; \emph{false refusal} rejects an available authorized positive-value purchase. \emph{Other} contains remaining value and execution failures. All outcomes remain in their cohort denominators. Primary DeepSeek \ours{} results count failed or unresolved execution as errors; other archived cohorts retain action--value scoring without requiring certified refusal (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). Comparisons are paired within configurations, with one semantic attempt per trial and bounded transport retries (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). The artifact provides the inputs, prompts, records, and offline evaluation tools; Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction} gives the reproduction map.

\subsection{Useful Completion at Authorization Boundaries}
\label{sec:boundaries}
All eleven primary configurations improve exactness and safe completion while reducing unsafe acceptances and false refusals (Figure~\ref{fig:completion}). For DeepSeek low/high, exact decisions rise from 248/400 (62.0\%) to 369/400 (92.25\%). The gain is 30.25 percentage points, with a 95\% template-cluster bootstrap interval of $[22.75,37.25]$, retaining both settings within each template. Exact purchases rise from 100 to 186 across 200 feasible outcomes; unsafe acceptances fall from 79 to 9 and false refusals from 53 to 1. Better risk control thus accompanies more completed tasks.

Gains cover all four contract families and 18 of 20 templates at each DeepSeek setting. They vary across models: GLM high improves from 64/200 to 188/200, Sol medium from 137/200 to 196/200, and Opus medium from 142/200 to 194/200. Qwen Max low rises from 59 to 87 exact outcomes but retains 46 unsafe acceptances. Appendix~\ref{app:statistics} gives complete partitions and template results. The paired design measures boundary responsiveness; its single feasible offer does not establish general ranking performance.

\subsection{Testing the Mechanisms behind Safe Choices}
\label{sec:permissions}
\label{sec:components}
\paragraph{Changing candidate availability.}
We examine 648 saved decision specifications from our framework, pooled across 72 tasks under DeepSeek low, high, and max. Each is replayed on mixed and unsafe-only catalogues, holding its conditions and selector fixed without further model calls. Valid mixed-catalogue executions select no unauthorized offer, although 23 specifications admit one. When only the unauthorized offer remains, eight select it (Figure~\ref{fig:ablations}). Each catalogue condition has 12 invalid executions. These changes expose existing permission errors, as predicted by the availability mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}.

\paragraph{Changing permission or value.}
Table~\ref{tab:oracle} examines 16 faulty Set outputs selected from one ambiguous ticket request. They omit Li's refund conditions and utility binding, yet select safe offers. Replacing only utility with the reference makes all 16 select an unauthorized offer. Replacing permission alone repairs the domain but leaves the value error. Both-component replacement or restoration of the omitted state's full semantics recovers exact decisions. Duplicating the remaining state's label changes nothing.

The utility intervention changes the rank of an offer that was already permitted. It demonstrates why correcting value cannot substitute for checking authorization. These reference-assisted interventions test the mechanism on selected faulty outputs; they are neither an automatic repair method nor a prevalence estimate (Appendix~\ref{app:permissionaudit}).

\subsection{Sensitivity and Reuse}
\label{sec:robustness}
On the 60-task reasoning panel, Set improves exactness in 17 of 18 complete Sol/Terra/Luna settings (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}a). Luna with reasoning disabled declines from 25/60 to 14/60. At medium, high, and max, Sol reaches 60/60; Terra reaches 59/60 at xhigh and max. These requested settings change the reliability of the generated conditions while the evaluator stays fixed. They do not measure equal computation across models.

The language study preserves identifiers, quantities, events, and authorization while changing Chinese/English wording. Across the six DeepSeek setting--language cells, Direct makes 33--36 exact decisions out of 60, and Set makes 55--59 (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}b). All gains lie between 21 and 25. Appendix~\ref{app:execution} reports complete results, the textual-checklist comparison, and output-budget controls.

\label{sec:reuse}
With requirements and attribute meanings fixed, the generated conditions can be reused across catalogues. Over two requirements with eight snapshots each, Astra uses two compilations and 22,160 tokens versus 16 Direct calls and 175,429 tokens, with 16/16 exact decisions for both. The 87.4\% workflow-level reduction includes compilation. Every new catalogue is evaluated again; changed authorization requires new conditions. Appendix~\ref{app:cost} reports the resource accounting.

\section{Conclusion}
Risk-aware delegation requires a common authorized purchase, not necessarily a unique interpretation of the request. Set evaluates each candidate's authorization and worst-case value across retained interpretations and contractual events. Across controlled contractual boundaries, the method improves useful completion while reducing unsafe acceptance and false refusal. Our analysis and interventions show why a safe selected purchase does not establish authorization-domain soundness: omitted states can weaken permission and inflate value, while availability and ranking can conceal the permission error. Assessing both the chosen purchase and the alternatives a rule permits gives a more informative account of whether the delegation has been respected.
\label{mainend}

\section*{Limitations}
The evaluated contracts are synthetic, with complete catalogues, known attributes, correct item grounding, and finite state sets. Risk limits and utility are supplied as inputs. The main benchmark isolates authorization boundaries. Its unique feasible candidate provides limited evidence about ranking among several admissible purchases. Intent tests signal unresolved readings explicitly. The evaluation does not cover open-ended interpretation discovery, hidden merchant terms, incomplete inventory, fraud, or settlement.

Decision conditions can omit a relevant requirement or bind it to the wrong state, even when the resulting specification executes successfully. Reference implementations check consistency with stipulated semantics and share a development workflow. Configuration, language, and diagnostic panels overlap and do not provide independent task samples. Catalogue visibility and provider compute differ across some controls. The appendix specifies each cohort's execution and scoring protocol, including reference-assisted interventions. The reported gains concern the complete workflow. The selected-output interventions establish specific error mechanisms, not their frequency across requests or deployed systems.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
All purchases are synthetic, with no monetary transfers or customer records. Financial consequences follow stipulated benchmark contracts. Saved artifacts distinguish original responses, reference-assisted interventions, and subsequent analysis. In deployment, erroneous acceptance could cause financial loss, while erroneous refusal could prevent access to a needed service. A deployed system would need explicit authorization and failure handling. Correct execution alone cannot establish that a compiled specification faithfully represents the user's request.

\section{Decision Semantics and Implementation Details}
\label{app:proofs}
\subsection{Decision semantics and assumptions}
RAC supplies the fixed-set risk-averse decision principle \citep{rac}. The target decision problem fixes $x=(P,c)$, a nonempty retained state set $\Theta(x)$, and state-dependent hard requirements, risk permissions, and utilities. Utilities share a cardinal scale and are bounded over the finite experimental domain. An execution guarantee applies to this target only if the compiled specification preserves its decision-relevant semantics. End-to-end evaluation retains compilation errors. The formulation requires neither a unique reading nor assumptions about state probabilities, linearity, or convexity. We do not implement conformal coverage or action-conditional calibration.

\label{app:ambiguity}
The ticket trace in Table~\ref{tab:observed-trace} illustrates these semantics: refunding both travellers is compatible with either retained reading without resolving the referent or expanding the budget.

\subsection{Generated conditions and evaluation}
\label{app:interface}
The Set implementation uses a small expression language to encode the conditions used by Algorithm~\ref{alg:decision}. A specification $Q$ contains state-indexed clauses $(\widehat H_j,\widehat R_j,\widehat u_j)$. Each state record contains a label and expressions \texttt{hard}, \texttt{risk}, and \texttt{utility}. Boolean expressions support conjunction, disjunction, negation, comparisons, and conditionals. Arithmetic supports exact literals, basic operations, minimum, maximum, and absolute value. Boolean and numeric values have distinct types. Expression variables refer to attributes in the schema. A missing attribute needed for a feasibility check leaves that check uncertified. The primary benchmark provides complete attributes.

For generated states $j=1,\ldots,m$, evaluation gives
\begin{align}
\widehat f(z)&=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat H_j(z)\wedge\widehat R_j(z)),\\
\widehat\psi(z)&=\min_{1\le j\le m}\widehat u_j(z).
\end{align}
The fixed procedure first requires complete enumeration of $C$, a decided $\widehat f(z)$ for every candidate, and known state utilities whenever $\widehat f(z)=1$. An aggregate predicate with a known false conjunct is false even if another conjunct is unknown. If these conditions cannot be established, the procedure returns \textsc{Fail}(uncertified) before selecting a purchase. The evaluator forms $\widehat F_C=\{z\in C:\widehat f(z)=1\}$ and computes the full maximizing set $Z^\star$. The response protocol and scorer permit any nonempty subset of tied positive-value maximizers with the correct guaranteed value. These are alternative complete purchases, of which one is selected. An empty domain or nonpositive maximum produces refusal. Invalid expressions produce failure rather than a certified refusal. Equivalent merged state expressions may encode the same decision functions.

\paragraph{Exactness on the declared domain.}
After these checks, membership in $\widehat F_C$ is decided for every $z\in C$, and all values over that domain are known. Finite enumeration therefore returns the complete set of maximizers when the maximum is positive. Otherwise, an empty domain or a nonpositive maximum proves that the compiled problem has no positive-value option. No unresolved candidate is silently excluded from the optimization.

Primary DeepSeek results use this complete-domain evaluator. Other archived cohorts retain their recorded execution and outcome-scoring protocols (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

Concrete syntax, serialization, decoding, and runtime bounds are documented with the implementation in the artifact. The evaluation semantics and certification conditions above apply to the generated specification.

\subsection{Reference calculations and freezing}
A cash-flow ledger evaluates bitmask events with integer or rational arithmetic, and a separate expression implementation uses the rule engine. Before model evaluation, we compare candidate feasibility, worst loss, guaranteed utility, and final decisions. They agree on all 6,712,320 candidate--event checks. Twenty fresh attribute assignments per template are also checked on all 64 subsets, yielding $20\times20\times64=25{,}600$ checks, plus three hand-calculated threshold witnesses. Both implementations arose within one development workflow and may share a misunderstanding of a clause despite agreeing numerically.

Tasks, references, prompts, and evaluator code are frozen before model evaluation. Public inputs exclude construction seeds, reference matrices, labels, and designated safe identifiers. Candidate visibility follows Table~\ref{tab:visibility}.

\subsection{Common instructions and outcome protocol}
\label{app:implementation}
All primary routes receive the same substantive policy, summarized below in English. The frozen model-facing prompts retain their original language and are unchanged by this summary.
\begin{quote}\small
Retain applicable interpretations. Respect current authorization in every retained state. Evaluate hard requirements and the complete risk permission, then maximize the minimum state-dependent utility. Buy only for positive guaranteed value; otherwise refuse. Do not ask the user or change permissions.
\end{quote}
Direct returns a decision, allowed identifiers, and guaranteed utility. Set returns the state-wise expressions of Appendix~\ref{app:interface}. Exactness requires agreement with the reference decision and, for acceptance, a nonempty subset of authorized maximizers and correct value. Disjoint failures are unsafe acceptance, false refusal, and Other (remaining value or invalid-execution failures). An invalid execution is not a correct refusal. Outside the primary DeepSeek comparison, archived cohorts use recorded action-and-value agreement; an unresolved no-action outcome may match the reference without certifying infeasibility.

\section{Controlled Decisions under Retained Intent Ambiguity}
\label{app:intent}
\subsection{Task semantics and controlled inputs}
The frozen parameter-boundary campaign contains three families with unresolved user interpretations. We include all three and exclude five families whose requirements are conditional but unambiguous. Selection is based on task semantics. This retrospective study is separate from the primary benchmark of contractual events.

\paragraph{Ticket-holder reference.}
Two male travellers need tickets, and ``his'' ticket must be refundable with a fee below the authorized limit. Utility penalizes that same traveller's delay and refund fee. Both referents remain possible. Each reading binds the hard requirement, risk bound, and value function to the corresponding traveller.

\paragraph{Hotel reference and conditional binding.}
Two guests need correctly registered rooms. ``His room'' must permit cancellation at 18:00 if he arrives at or after 18:00, or 24 hours before his expected arrival otherwise. The cancellation fee and utility weights refer to the same guest, so the unresolved antecedent affects conditional requirements and value.

\paragraph{Distributive versus collective scope.}
Gifts must satisfy recipients' preferences and wrapping requirements. ``Buy one for each; the exchange top-up is at most $r$'' may bound each recipient or the batch. Stipulated utility uses the corresponding maximum individual or total top-up. With nonnegative charges, the batch bound may dominate the individual bounds. The representation must still preserve the bound and score for each reading.

All requests explicitly signal unresolved reference or scope. Methods receive identical user text, complete catalogue attributes, synthetic utility formulas, and the common decision policy. Compilation in this historical panel is catalogue-visible; the primary Set protocol excludes the catalogue. Models receive no reference expressions, answers, or subsequent user clarification. The study evaluates decisions under the signaled ambiguity and does not test automatic discovery of unrestricted interpretations.

\subsection{Parameter design and findings}
Three families, seven settings, and two frozen seeds give 42 API variants. Within each parameter group the candidates and identifiers stay fixed. The authorized risk limit takes values of 0, 500, and 1,000 subunits, and the risk penalty takes values of 1, 3, and 10. A value offset places the best common candidate at exactly zero or one. Each seed has 18 acceptance and three zero-value refusal variants. The refusal cases have a nonempty feasible intersection and test the value boundary. The second seed changes the instance within the same templates.
