\section{Introduction}
Language agents increasingly turn natural-language requests into actions through tools and external services \citep{react}. Shopping, travel-planning, and service benchmarks already test grounded choices, interacting requirements, and policy-governed workflows \citep{webshop,travelplanner,taubench}. Payment delegation adds a consequential decision: committing the user's resources to a purchase whose acceptability may depend on events that have not occurred. A purchase can meet its initial budget yet expose the user to an unacceptable cancellation or replacement cost. We study this setting with no further user interaction after the initial delegation. The agent must preserve the value of the delegation while respecting the requirements under which it may act.

Relevant uncertainty concerns both the meaning of a request and the consequences of a purchase. The request and available context may support several reasonable interpretations without specifying a unique intended reading; a contract may permit several future outcomes. Our setting excludes further user interaction and does not require either source of uncertainty to be resolved before acting. Computing the joint effect of conditional clauses adds a distinct difficulty. Selecting one interpretation or event prematurely can overlook an unacceptable consequence, while refusing solely because uncertainty remains can discard a purchase acceptable under every retained possibility. The central question is therefore: \emph{can the agent identify one useful purchase that remains admissible across the retained interpretations and outcomes?}

For ``his ticket must be refundable,'' two orders refunding different travellers satisfy the readings separately but provide no common purchase. An order refunding both can permit action within the existing budget and value conditions. Preserving ambiguity can change either acceptance or refusal without creating spending permission.

Uncertainty-aware planning and clarification methods determine when information should be requested \citep{knowno,intentsim,sage}. Value of Information balances the benefit of asking against interaction costs, including in flight-booking and e-commerce tasks \citep{voi}. Risk-Averse Calibration (RAC) relates uncertainty sets to consequence-sensitive action selection \citep{rac}, while program-aided and neurosymbolic reasoning separate interpretation from computation \citep{pal,logiclm,logicpy}. We connect these foundations to purchasing without further user response: construct state-dependent conditions from the request, then determine whether they permit a useful action.

Our approach retains decision-relevant possibilities and expresses what the \emph{same} purchase must satisfy in each. Hard requirements and authorized risk limits determine admissibility; utility ranks the admissible purchases. A favorable utility cannot compensate for a violated permission. Keeping these objects separate also enables a stronger evaluation than checking the selected action alone: a rule may select a safe purchase while permitting an unauthorized alternative that loses under the current ranking.

We present a \emph{state-wise executable framework} that binds requirements, financial permissions, and utility to the same retained interpretation or event. A language-model front end instantiates this predefined interface from the natural-language request, context, and attribute schema. Building on RAC's fixed-set max--min principle, a fixed evaluator checks joint admissibility and selects a positive-value maximizing purchase or refuses when none exists. The interface makes both the generated conditions and their consequences inspectable; semantic compilation errors remain part of the evaluated pipeline.

We evaluate this pipeline on 200 reference-checked tasks spanning 20 contractual structures. Across two DeepSeek configurations, exact decisions increase from 62.0\% to 92.5\%, while unsafe acceptances and false refusals both decrease. Separate tests examine explicitly signaled pronoun and scope ambiguity. Frozen-rule replays reveal authorization errors concealed by safe current choices; component interventions show that correcting utility alone can make an unauthorized purchase win. Cross-configuration and reuse studies examine the scope and cost of the workflow. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Risk-aware delegation semantics:} one purchase across retained interpretations and events, separating authorization, worst-case value, and justified refusal without requiring a unique reading or further interaction.
\item \textbf{An executable intent interface:} state-wise clauses that bind requirements, financial permissions, and utility, with predefined aggregation and selection and inspectable generated conditions.
\item \textbf{Controlled evaluation and semantic diagnostics:} paired boundaries and retained-intent tests of end-to-end decisions, plus frozen-rule and component interventions that distinguish correct choices from sound authorization domains.
\end{itemize}
