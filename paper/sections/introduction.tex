\section{Introduction}
Language agents increasingly turn natural-language requests into actions through tools and external services \citep{react}. Shopping, travel-planning, and service benchmarks already test grounded choices, interacting requirements, and policy-governed workflows \citep{webshop,travelplanner,taubench}. Payment delegation adds a consequential decision: committing the user's resources to a purchase whose acceptability may depend on events that have not occurred. A purchase can meet its initial budget yet expose the user to an unacceptable cancellation or replacement cost. We study this setting with no further user interaction after the initial delegation. The agent must preserve the value of the delegation while respecting the requirements under which it may act.

Relevant uncertainty concerns both the meaning of a request and the consequences of a purchase. The request and available context may support several reasonable interpretations without specifying a unique intended reading; a contract may permit several future outcomes. Our setting excludes further user interaction and does not require either source of uncertainty to be resolved before acting. Computing the joint effect of conditional clauses adds a distinct difficulty. Selecting one interpretation or event prematurely can overlook an unacceptable consequence, while refusing solely because uncertainty remains can discard a purchase acceptable under every retained possibility. The central question is therefore: \emph{can the agent identify one useful purchase that remains admissible across the retained interpretations and outcomes?}

Existing research provides complementary foundations. Uncertainty-aware planning and clarification methods determine when information should be requested \citep{knowno,intentsim,sage}. Value of Information explicitly balances the benefit of asking against interaction costs, including in flight-booking and e-commerce tasks \citep{voi}. Risk-Averse Calibration (RAC) relates uncertainty sets to consequence-sensitive action selection \citep{rac}, while program-aided and neurosymbolic reasoning separate language interpretation from computation \citep{pal,logiclm,logicpy}. These advances motivate a specific language-to-decision task: with no further user response, construct executable purchasing conditions from the request's requirements, conditional scope, and financial consequences, then determine whether those conditions permit a useful action.

Our approach retains decision-relevant possibilities and expresses what the \emph{same} purchase must satisfy in each. Hard requirements and authorized risk limits determine admissibility; utility ranks the admissible purchases. A favorable utility cannot compensate for a violated permission. Conversely, unresolved uncertainty is not itself a reason to refuse: an admissible purchase with positive guaranteed value remains actionable. This separation connects the meaning of the user's delegation to both successful completion and justified refusal. The representation must carry the retained alternatives and their consequences into the decision process.

We present a \emph{state-wise executable framework} for risk-aware payment delegation under unresolved uncertainty. Its predefined representation associates decision-relevant interpretations and outcomes with state-dependent requirements, risk conditions, and utility. This carries unresolved ambiguity into purchasing: one action must remain admissible across the retained possibilities, while its value is assessed by worst-case utility. Building on RAC's fixed-set max--min principle, a fixed procedure selects a positive-value maximizing purchase or abstains when none exists. Our implementation uses a language-model front end to instantiate the representation from the request, context, and attribute schema, followed by deterministic evaluation over candidate offers.

We evaluate this approach on 200 reference-checked tasks spanning 20 contractual structures with explicit event uncertainty. Across two DeepSeek configurations, exact decisions increase from 62.0\% to 92.5\%, while unsafe acceptances and false refusals both decrease. Separate controlled tests examine retained pronoun and scope ambiguity; reference interventions trace failures to omitted interpretations. Cross-model, effort, language, and reuse studies examine reliability and cost. These complementary studies support useful decisions within explicit risk boundaries. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Risk-aware payment delegation:} a language-to-decision formulation that retains decision-relevant alternatives and separates admissibility, guaranteed value, and justified refusal without further user interaction.
\item \textbf{An executable payment-decision framework:} an inspectable state-wise interface linking retained alternatives to requirements, financial permissions, and utility, with fixed decision semantics and reusable catalogue-independent specifications.
\item \textbf{Controlled reliability evaluation:} reference-checked paired payment boundaries, cross-configuration studies, and semantic diagnostics that measure safe completion, missed feasible purchases, and remaining compilation failures.
\end{itemize}
